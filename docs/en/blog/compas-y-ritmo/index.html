Beat and rhythm

Life doesn't need a perfect routine, but a structure to come back to

Not long ago, someone very special said something to me in a conversation that stayed with me: life is a matter of beat and rhythm.

At first I thought we were talking only about habits: getting up early, exercising, eating well, sleeping, writing, praying. All those things that, when they’re in order, seem to keep life in order. But the more I thought about it, the more I understood that beat and rhythm couldn’t be reduced to a perfect routine. Life isn’t linear. Relationships change, work changes, the economy, our mood, our body, even the questions we ask ourselves. A structure that can’t adapt to those changes ends up being another form of rigidity.

The beat, for me, is something deeper: it’s the inner structure that orients us. The rhythm is the way we move forward within it.

We can change the speed without losing the beat. We can change our habits without betraying their principles. There are seasons for moving ahead with intensity and others for recovering, waiting, or doing less. The problem isn’t slowing down. The problem is no longer knowing where we’re headed, or starting to move frantically just so we won’t feel like we’re standing still.

A lot of movement isn’t always progress

For a long time I’ve confused activity with progress. When anxiety shows up, because of a money problem, an emotional one or a work one, my mind starts producing solutions. One idea, then another, and then another. Each one can be reasonable on its own. The problem is that I haven’t started the first one and I’m already trying to solve the fifth.

So I do more, but I move forward less.

Being busy can become a very convincing anesthetic. It lets us feel like we’re solving something when, in reality, we’re avoiding choosing. And not choosing has an emotional advantage: as long as every possibility stays open, none of them has failed yet. But it also has a cost: energy gets scattered, what’s important gets less attention, and anxiety grows because, despite all the movement, reality doesn’t change.

Rhythm isn’t measured by how many things I do, but by the evidence that I’m moving forward on what I decided matters.

If I want to reduce a debt, the debt has to start going down. If I want to recover my health, some indicator of my health has to improve. If I want to strengthen a relationship, my actions have to build trust. Intention matters, but it doesn’t replace reality. A beat without measurement can turn into a story; a rhythm without correction can turn into repetition.

A simple tool has helped me land my ideas. I write the central issue inside a box and, around it, I draw circles with concrete actions. The box reminds me of the purpose; the actions give it movement. What matters isn’t producing a map full of tasks, but identifying the next action that depends on me and checking whether it really moves what I want to change.

Awareness recovers the beat. Action recovers the rhythm.

Separating the facts from the story

When anxiety rises, the mind stops being a reliable observer. It mixes what happened with what it fears will happen, turns a possibility into a certainty, and then makes decisions to defend itself against a story that may not be real yet.

That’s why I’ve learned to ask myself four questions:

  1. What happened? Only the facts.

  2. What is my mind telling me about what happened?

  3. What am I feeling?

  4. Is there another way of looking at this situation?

Sometimes I add three more: what do I know and what am I assuming?, what really depends on me?, what is the next small, verifiable action?

The intention isn’t to deny the emotion. What I feel is real, even if the interpretation that produced it may be incomplete. Separating facts, stories and emotions lets me listen to them without handing them, indiscriminately, the government of my conduct.

I’ve started to recognize my own signs of losing the beat: I think in loops, anxiety goes up, new projects appear, my sleep gets worse, I feel uneasy, and my decisions become imprecise. I used to read the arrival of new ideas as a solution. Today, at certain moments, I’m starting to see it as an early sign of disorganization.

When several of those signs show up, maybe it isn’t the time to open another project, take on a major financial commitment, or send a final message. First you have to go back to the center: talk, write, sleep, tell the facts apart from the story, and choose a single action that is under our control.

Coherence starts before promising

I’ve also come to understand that coherence between thinking, saying, feeling and doing doesn’t mean those four will always line up on their own. I can feel fear and act with courage. I can have doubts and still meet a responsibility. I can be sad and keep taking care of myself. Coherence doesn’t require perfect emotions; it requires that a conscious intention govern my conduct.

This becomes especially hard when the need to please shows up. Many times we say yes so we won’t disappoint anyone, keep a bond, avoid being abandoned, or prove we’re capable. Then we try to honor our word, but we discover that the promise was born of fear and that keeping it requires abandoning ourselves.

Coherence doesn’t begin when we keep what we promised. It begins when we decide what to promise and from what place we do it.

That’s why, sometimes, the most honest answer is: “Let me think about it.” And when we’ve already promised something we can’t deliver, integrity doesn’t mean hiding or holding on to it blindly at any cost. It means admitting it early, speaking clearly, reducing the harm, and taking on the consequences of correcting course.

There’s another difficulty: holding a limit when the other person turns us into the bad guy of their story. When someone gets used to receiving our help, they may read the limit as an attack. When we collect a debt, they may act as if the problem were the collecting and not the default. When we stop carrying someone else’s responsibility, they may accuse us of selfishness.

The other person’s discomfort doesn’t prove our limit is unjust. But feeling uncomfortable isn’t enough to conclude we’re right, either. A limit has to be checked against the facts, the responsibility, the proportionality, and, when the emotion is too intense, the eyes of someone trustworthy. Once it’s been reviewed, we have to learn to hold it even if it isn’t approved.

A limit that I can only keep while the other person accepts it isn’t a limit yet.

Resting is also listening

For a long time I didn’t know how to be alone. I didn’t really know what I liked because I was used to organizing myself around other people’s tastes and needs. Solitude felt like abandonment.

Over time, though, it began to turn into something else: a space to get to know myself. I started to enjoy my own rituals: the morning coffee, the rosary, prayer, writing, exercise, sleep, podcasts and reading. They aren’t extraordinary activities. Their value is that they let me come back to myself.

Rest, then, isn’t only sleeping or stopping work. It’s also ceasing, for a moment, to answer to other people’s expectations so I can listen to what I want, what I like, and how I want to live.

Rest doesn’t interrupt the rhythm of life; it lets us hear what our real rhythm is.

Learning to be alone doesn’t mean giving up company. It means no longer needing it in order to know who I am. And maybe only from there is it possible to accompany others without disappearing inside their wishes.

Firm principles, flexible habits

Not all habits work for all seasons. A practice can sustain us at one time and increase the pressure on us at another. That’s why discipline shouldn’t be measured by the rigid repetition of a routine, but by the ability to keep the purpose while we adapt the form.

Habits are instruments. Principles are the direction.

In my case, I’m starting to recognize at least four principles:

  • Not abandoning myself: including my well-being, my wishes and my limits in my decisions.
  • Acting from virtue and not from fear: not letting anguish, rejection or the need for approval govern.
  • Creating value without extracting it unjustly: seeking responsible, reciprocal and proportionate relationships and decisions.
  • Looking at reality with awareness: telling facts, interpretations and emotions apart before acting.

Putting myself first doesn’t mean doing only what I like or waiting until I’m completely sorted out to relate to others. It means that loving, serving or accompanying others no longer requires me to abandon myself.

Faith tunes the beat

In this process, spirituality has a central place. In God, in Jesus, in the Virgin, in the angels and in prayer I find peace, trust and company. But prayer isn’t only comfort. It’s also a place of honesty.

Before God there are no masks. When I pray, I can examine an intention and ask myself whether I’m acting with justice, from virtue or from fear. Prayer helps me discern which action is mine to take and, once I’ve taken it, to let go of what I can no longer control.

Faith doesn’t guarantee that everything will happen the way I want. It lets me trust that I’ll be able to go through whatever happens accompanied and without giving up on doing right. Prayer doesn’t replace action: it cleans the intention, guides the action, and lets me hand over the result.

Stepping back into the music

Living with beat and rhythm doesn’t mean never falling out of order. In music, missing a beat doesn’t mean you have to stop the song. You listen again and step into the next measure.

Maybe the goal isn’t to build a life where anxiety, sadness or confusion never show up. Maybe it’s learning to recognize the signs sooner, to shorten the time we stay lost, and to have a simple structure to come back to: talk to someone, write, take care of the body, review the facts, remember the principles, and take the next right action.

I don’t need to have all my habits perfectly organized, or force life to behave in a straight line. I need principles clear enough to adapt to each season without losing myself; a structure that reconnects what I think, what I feel and what I do; and the humility to correct course when I discover I’ve fallen out of the beat.

The beat doesn’t keep life from changing. It lets me change with it without ceasing to be who I am and who I want to be.

And rhythm isn’t about doing everything or doing it fast. It’s about continuing to move forward, with intention and awareness, toward what truly matters.
