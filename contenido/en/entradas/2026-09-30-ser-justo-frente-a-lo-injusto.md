I've been thinking a lot about justice.

It's a word we use easily when we feel someone treated us badly, broke their word, took advantage of our trust, or made us carry consequences that weren't ours. In those moments we say: this is unjust. And many times we're right.

But there is a harder question: how do I act justly when someone has been unjust to me, or is being unjust to me right now?

There, justice stops being an accusation against the other person and becomes a demand on myself.

**Justice as a virtue**

For the Stoics, justice was one of the four cardinal virtues, along with wisdom, courage and temperance. It wasn't only about obeying the law. It was a way of relating to others from reason, truth, shared dignity and the good of the community.

Human beings, for them, weren't made to live in isolation. We are social beings. We depend on each other, we hold different roles, and our decisions always end up affecting someone else. That's why Marcus Aurelius kept reminding himself that human beings exist for one another and that our actions should have a social purpose.

Stoic justice demands telling the truth, keeping your word, respecting agreements, not using people merely as a means, and taking responsibility for the consequences of your own decisions. It also demands that we fulfill well the roles we accepted: as family, friends, citizens, leaders, colleagues or partners.

It doesn't only ask “what is owed to me?” It also asks: “what do I owe?”, “what responsibility did I take on?” and “what effect does my conduct have on others?”

**Justice is not passivity**

There's a caricature of Stoicism in which being Stoic means putting up with things in silence, accepting any abuse, and repeating that nothing external matters. That reading doesn't convince me, and it doesn't represent Stoic philosophy well either. Although I confess it happens to me again and again.

Accepting that something happened doesn't mean approving of it. Recognizing that I can't change the past doesn't oblige me to trust again, stay in a relationship, give up a claim, or tolerate the harm continuing.

I can demand that an agreement be honored, ask for repair, end a relationship, take back a responsibility, go to the institutions, or set a consequence. All of that can be compatible with justice.

The difference is in the purpose and in the manner.

Repair seeks to restore what is right. Revenge seeks to make the other person suffer because I suffered. Repair sets limits so the harm doesn't happen again. Revenge wants to hand the pain back, even if to do it I have to become something I also consider unjust.

Justice doesn't ask me to stay still. It asks that the means I use also be just.

**Anger can't be both judge and executioner**

Injustice produces anger. It would be naive to pretend it doesn't.

Anger can serve a purpose: it warns us that someone crossed a line, failed a duty, or violated something we value. The problem starts when that alarm also becomes judge, strategist and executioner.

In the middle of anger we mix facts with interpretations. Someone broke a commitment and we conclude their whole being is bad. Someone acted irresponsibly and we turn them forever into an irresponsible person. We hear one version, fill in what we don't know, and end up acting against a story that may contain some truth, but also fear, pride and pain.

Being just requires separating:

- What happened and I can verify.
- What third parties told me.
- What I interpret about the other person's intentions.
- The total judgment I make about their character.

A behavior can be dishonest, abusive or irresponsible, and it should be called by its name. But condemning the whole person rarely improves our ability to decide. Precision doesn't weaken the claim; it makes it stronger.

I don't need to stop feeling anger in order to act well. I need to keep anger from deciding for me.

**Owning my part without carrying the other's**

When an injustice repeats, it's easy to end up at one of two extremes. In the first, all the blame is the other person's and I have nothing to look at. In the second, I blame myself for having trusted, for not setting limits, or for not foreseeing what happened, and I end up absorbing the responsibility of the person who chose to act badly.

Neither extreme is just.

I can recognize that I was ambiguous, that I put off a conversation, that I accepted an agreement without enough clarity, or that I offered more than I could really sustain. That's my responsibility. But I'm not responsible for someone else lying, failing to deliver, or deciding to take advantage of a lack of limits.

Having left a door open doesn't make me responsible for someone deciding to walk through it.

Justice requires assigning responsibility correctly: taking on fully what is mine and handing back to the other person what belongs to them. No less, no more.

This distinction matters especially for those of us who tend to solve everything. We say “I'll take care of it,” we take on other people's obligations, we avoid uncomfortable conversations, and we confuse being generous with making ourselves indispensable. Sometimes we do it out of love. Other times, out of fear of disappointing, of not being enough, or of someone leaving.

Later, when it becomes unsustainable, we feel the other person took advantage. And it may be true. But we also have to admit that we fed a dynamic in which giving replaced talking and fixing replaced setting limits.

Recognizing this doesn't excuse the other person. It gives us back the ability to act differently.

**Generosity also needs justice**

I've always thought generosity is a virtue. I still believe it. But I've started to understand that not everything that looks generous really is.

Just generosity is born of freedom. I give because I want to, not because I'm afraid. I give because I can, not to prove that I can. I help without going against my own well-being and without taking away the other person's responsibility for their own life.

If I give to keep from being abandoned, my giving carries a hidden expectation. If I offer something I can't sustain, sooner or later failure to deliver and resentment will show up. If I always fix other people's consequences, maybe I'm not helping them: I may be keeping them from developing judgment, autonomy and responsibility.

Clarity matters too. If something is a gift, it should be given freely and not charged for emotionally afterward. If it's an agreement, a partnership, a financial obligation or conditional help, it needs terms, limits, dates and consequences.

Many wounds aren't born only from having given too much. They're born from never having defined what we were giving, for how long, and what responsibility the other person kept.

For me, a just generosity could be summed up like this:

> Give because I want to, not because I'm afraid. Give because I can, not to prove that I can. Help without abandoning myself and without taking from the other person the dignity of answering for their own life.

**Setting limits is not acting against someone**

Sometimes we believe that setting a limit is acting against the other person. But a clear limit can be more respectful than ambiguous help sustained with resentment.

Saying “this far I can” acknowledges reality. Saying “this is yours” acknowledges the other person's capacity. Saying “I will no longer take part in this dynamic” is not necessarily abandoning; it can be ceasing to maintain a relationship built on badly distributed responsibilities.

Limits also keep our supposed goodness from turning into a form of control. Someone who solves everything can end up expecting obedience, gratitude, loyalty or love in return. Then the help is no longer completely free.

Loving all human beings doesn't require trusting all of them. Forgiving doesn't oblige you to rebuild a relationship. Understanding someone's limitations doesn't mean handing back to them what they've already shown they don't know how to take care of.

Justice can keep its goodwill and, at the same time, adjust closeness, authority and trust to the evidence.

**Justice needs the other virtues**

Justice never acts alone.

It needs wisdom to tell facts from impressions and to decide what would really be proportionate. It needs courage to confront, to claim, or to end something even when it has a cost. It needs temperance so I don't exaggerate, humiliate, threaten, or act from a desire for revenge.

Without wisdom, I can call injustice any outcome I don't like. Without courage, I recognize what's unjust but keep allowing it. Without temperance, I answer harm by causing more harm. And without justice, I use my intelligence, my strength and my resources only to defend what suits me.

That union of the virtues makes the Stoic answer much more demanding than simply “letting it go.” It obliges me to act, but it also obliges me to look after who I become while I act.

**What depends on me**

Epictetus insisted on distinguishing between what depends on us and what doesn't. In the face of an injustice, it doesn't entirely depend on me that the other person acknowledges the harm, complies voluntarily, asks for forgiveness, or considers me a just person.

What does depend on me is telling the truth, documenting the facts, making a proportionate request, choosing legitimate means, setting limits, and following through on the consequences I announced. It depends on me not to promise what I know I can't deliver. It depends on me not to buy peace at the cost of my own stability.

Acting justly doesn't guarantee that I'll receive justice. That may be one of the hardest truths of Stoicism.

You can do everything right and still lose money, a relationship, an opportunity or a reputation. Virtue doesn't control the outcome. It controls the way we take part in it.

Accepting this limit doesn't mean giving up. It means doing everything reasonable and legitimate without also handing over our peace, our character and the rest of our life to an outcome that doesn't depend entirely on us.

**Not collaborating again with my own abandonment**

When I began this reflection I thought justice was mainly about correcting what others had done wrong. Now I believe it also means reviewing the conditions under which I myself take part in my relationships.

Not to blame myself for someone else's conduct. Not to say I deserved what happened. But to recognize the signs that show up before the pattern repeats: acting from fear, wanting to fix the other person's life, feeling like a savior, offering more than is sustainable, and leaving agreements and limits undefined.

My lesson can't be to stop trusting, stop loving, or stop being generous. That would be letting someone else's injustice make my character poorer.

The lesson has to be a different one: trust with evidence, love without buying permanence, help without taking over responsibility, and give without abandoning myself.

When someone acts unjustly toward me, being just doesn't mean putting up with it, agreeing with them, buying peace at my own expense, or giving up on demanding repair. It means owning my part without carrying the other's responsibility, claiming firmly what is owed, setting clear limits, and not letting anger turn me into the very thing I'm condemning.

Justice isn't only about not harming the other person.

It's also about ceasing to abandon myself so that the other person will choose me, won't get angry, or won't leave.

Maybe that's the hardest test: working to correct what is unjust without losing my own virtue in the attempt.

**Reading notes**

This reflection is inspired mainly by Marcus Aurelius's Meditations, especially his ideas about the social nature of human beings, actions aimed at the common good, and the need to act justly even toward unjust people; by Epictetus's Discourses and Enchiridion, particularly the distinction between what depends on us and what doesn't; and by the Stoic idea of oikeíōsis, according to which care widens from oneself outward to family, community and humanity.
